# Water Quality

Water is one of the most fundamental natural resources and a crucial factor in sustaining life on Earth. It plays a vital role in human health, ecosystem preservation, agricultural production, industrial activities, and societal development. Despite the abundance of water on the Earth's surface, only a fraction of these resources is suitable for various human uses, and the quality of these sources can be altered by natural factors, human activities, and environmental conditions.

Water quality refers to the set of physical, chemical, and microbiological characteristics that determine the water's suitability for a specific use. These characteristics extend beyond mere visual clarity; factors such as odor, taste, color, dissolved mineral content, acidity, hardness, turbidity, and the presence of contaminants are also critical to its assessment. For instance, water might appear clear and taste natural yet still contain invisible contaminants or pathogenic microorganisms. Consequently, assessing water quality cannot be achieved solely through observation, smelling, or tasting.

Regarding physical characteristics, factors such as color, odor, taste, and turbidity can influence consumer acceptance. Changes in odor or taste may stem from the presence of minerals, organic compounds, disinfectants, or certain contaminants; however, these indicators alone are insufficient to identify the underlying cause or determine the water's safety. Turbidity can indicate the presence of suspended particles and, under certain conditions, affect the efficacy of treatment and disinfection processes. From a chemical perspective, water-soluble compounds and salts play a crucial role in determining water's characteristics and applications. Substances such as calcium and magnesium influence water hardness; sodium, chloride, and sulfate affect taste and total dissolved solids; and pH levels indicate whether the water is acidic or alkaline. Furthermore, the presence or concentration of elements and compounds—such as nitrates, heavy metals, and other chemical contaminants—must be evaluated against the intended use and relevant standards. It is important to note that the presence of minerals in water does not necessarily indicate contamination; the significance of any substance depends on its type, concentration, and the water's intended application.

In addition to physical and chemical properties, the microbiological quality of water is of particular importance. The presence of certain pathogenic bacteria, viruses, and parasites can pose risks to consumer health. Consequently, the assessment of drinking water must rely on a suite of validated tests; safety cannot be determined solely by examining taste, odor, appearance, or mineral content.

Given the vital role of water in daily life and public health, measuring and comparing water quality indicators has become essential for distinguishing between water sources, assessing compliance with relevant standards, and identifying factors that influence water quality. Today, laboratory data and statistical analysis methods enable the examination of various water parameters, the identification of data patterns, and the study of potential correlations between physical and chemical properties. In this project, by examining and analyzing various water quality indicators, we aim to gain a clearer picture of the water samples' characteristics, identify differences between them, and determine which parameters are most significant in describing water quality. The results of this analysis can contribute to a better understanding of the samples' status; however, conclusively determining the water's potability requires a proper assessment of all essential indicators against recognized standards.

# The features of the dataset are:
1. pH value:
PH is an important parameter in evaluating the acid–base balance of water. It is also the indicator of acidic or alkaline condition of water status. WHO has recommended maximum permissible limit of pH from 6.5 to 8.5. The current investigation ranges were 6.52–6.83 which are in the range of WHO standards.

2. Hardness:
Hardness is mainly caused by calcium and magnesium salts. These salts are dissolved from geologic deposits through which water travels. The length of time water is in contact with hardness producing material helps determine how much hardness there is in raw water. Hardness was originally defined as the capacity of water to precipitate soap caused by Calcium and Magnesium.

3. Solids (Total dissolved solids - TDS):
Water has the ability to dissolve a wide range of inorganic and some organic minerals or salts such as potassium, calcium, sodium, bicarbonates, chlorides, magnesium, sulfates etc. These minerals produced un-wanted taste and diluted color in appearance of water. This is the important parameter for the use of water. The water with high TDS value indicates that water is highly mineralized. Desirable limit for TDS is 500 mg/l and maximum limit is 1000 mg/l which prescribed for drinking purpose.

4. Chloramines:
Chlorine and chloramine are the major disinfectants used in public water systems. Chloramines are most commonly formed when ammonia is added to chlorine to treat drinking water. Chlorine levels up to 4 milligrams per liter (mg/L or 4 parts per million (ppm)) are considered safe in drinking water.

5. Sulfate:
Sulfates are naturally occurring substances that are found in minerals, soil, and rocks. They are present in ambient air, groundwater, plants, and food. The principal commercial use of sulfate is in the chemical industry. Sulfate concentration in seawater is about 2,700 milligrams per liter (mg/L). It ranges from 3 to 30 mg/L in most freshwater supplies, although much higher concentrations (1000 mg/L) are found in some geographic locations.

6. Conductivity:
Pure water is not a good conductor of electric current rather’s a good insulator. Increase in ions concentration enhances the electrical conductivity of water. Generally, the amount of dissolved solids in water determines the electrical conductivity. Electrical conductivity (EC) actually measures the ionic process of a solution that enables it to transmit current. According to WHO standards, EC value should not exceeded 400 μS/cm.

7. Organic_carbon:
Total Organic Carbon (TOC) in source waters comes from decaying natural organic matter (NOM) as well as synthetic sources. TOC is a measure of the total amount of carbon in organic compounds in pure water. According to US EPA < 2 mg/L as TOC in treated / drinking water, and < 4 mg/Lit in source water which is use for treatment.

8. Trihalomethanes:
THMs are chemicals which may be found in water treated with chlorine. The concentration of THMs in drinking water varies according to the level of organic material in the water, the amount of chlorine required to treat the water, and the temperature of the water that is being treated. THM levels up to 80 ppm is considered safe in drinking water.

9. Turbidity:
The turbidity of water depends on the quantity of solid matter present in the suspended state. It is a measure of light emitting properties of water and the test is used to indicate the quality of waste discharge with respect to colloidal matter. The mean turbidity value obtained for Wondo Genet Campus (0.98 NTU) is lower than the WHO recommended value of 5.00 NTU.

10. Potability:
Indicates if water is safe for human consumption where 1 means Potable and 0 means Not potable.
